Amazon Fresh · 2026 · Climate Impact Design Challenge

Smart Bundles: a predictive cart system that reduces household food waste by default

Role Product Designer (Solo)
Timeline 10 hours
Focus AI-driven decision support · Behavioral systems · Climate-positive defaults
Outcome High-fidelity prototype across 3 touchpoints
Explore Prototype See deck

Overview

The cart is optimized for checkout velocity — not decision quality.

Household food waste is a systems problem hiding inside everyday shopping defaults. Digital grocery makes it easy to over-buy perishables, duplicate ingredients, and lose track of what will actually get used. Smart Bundles reframes Amazon Fresh as a decision-support system: using behavioral signals to right-size quantities, group items into complete-use bundles, and learn from lightweight feedback after delivery.

Design challenge

Design a climate-positive upgrade inside an existing digital ecosystem — without changing how people shop or cook.

Constraint

10-hour sprint → prototype only what matters at system scale: the closed loop (suggest → purchase → learn).

System bet

Reduce waste by shifting defaults (quantities + grouping) at the point of decision — the cart — instead of relying on user willpower later.

System at a glance

Inputs Cart contents, purchase history, household size, consumption rhythm, perishability windows, ingredient overlap
Intelligence Predicts over-purchase risk + proposes right-sized quantities and bundle groupings that close ingredient loops
Outputs Bundle cards, quantity guidance, waste-avoidance estimates, post-delivery feedback prompts
Feedback loop Feedback updates future bundle sizing — improving accuracy without extra effort

Solution

Portion-controlled bundling that stays visible through checkout — and gets smarter after delivery.

1 · Right-sized quantities

Suggests smaller portions when historical consumption indicates over-buy risk — with a one-line rationale and easy editing.

2 · Bundle grouping

Groups complementary items into complete-use sets (e.g., weeknight chicken + veg + grain), reducing orphan ingredients.

3 · Lightweight learning

After delivery, a quick optional prompt captures what was “too much” or “too little,” improving future recommendations.

Core Flows

Three touchpoints that prove the system.

Instead of designing a full end-to-end grocery experience in 10 hours, I prioritized the three places where the system must be “felt” by users: discoverability (make guidance visible by default), checkout continuity (keep bundles intact), and learning (improve without effort).

Touchpoint 1: Make quantity guidance visible by default

Smart Bundles surfaces pre-portioned bundles and right-sized quantities before the cart. This is where the system earns trust: by making the sustainable choice feel like the obvious choice.

  • Default-first: bundles appear as a primary shopping mode, not a buried “sustainability” feature.
  • Explainability: each bundle communicates how it matches typical cooking patterns (meals / servings) — not just price.
  • Flexible control: users can edit any item quantity; the system adapts, it doesn’t enforce.

Touchpoint 2: Keep bundles grouped through checkout

The cart is where waste-risk decisions happen — duplicate perishables, oversized proteins, or missing ingredients that create partial-use leftovers. Smart Bundles keeps grouped items intact and makes edits frictionless.

  • Bundle integrity: items stay grouped as a unit so users understand the “complete-use” logic.
  • One-tap edits: adjust quantities without breaking the bundle structure.
  • Outcome visibility: shows a lightweight “waste avoided” estimate to reinforce behavior without guilt.
PLACEHOLDER — Checkout friction audit

Touchpoint 3: Learn without asking for effort

Most waste happens after delivery — outside the system. Smart Bundles closes the loop with an optional, low-friction prompt that directly improves future portion sizing.

  • Optional by design: no nagging; feedback is a single-tap interaction.
  • Trust reinforcement: shows exactly what changed (“We’ll suggest slightly smaller portions next time”).
  • Behavioral realism: reduces waste without requiring strict meal planning.

Research

Waste is behavioral — and concentrated.

I anchored the concept in a simple insight: broad awareness campaigns don’t move the needle if the system’s defaults keep producing over-purchase. Research indicated that food waste is not evenly distributed — the highest-waste segment drives disproportionate waste, especially from high-impact items like proteins.

Climate impact

Food loss and waste account for ~8–10% of annual global greenhouse gas emissions.

Household scale

30–40% of the U.S. food supply is wasted — with households as the largest contributor.

Economic impact

U.S. households waste ~$1,300+ per year in uneaten food.

System Logic

Designing the intelligence layer — not just the UI.

A Google-level product concept needs a coherent system model: what signals are used, where decisions happen, and how the system gets better over time. Smart Bundles is intentionally structured as a closed loop that turns a one-time cart suggestion into compounding accuracy across orders.

PLACEHOLDER DIAGRAM — Input → Model → Output
Key system constraint

Interventions must be non-blocking. The model advises, but the user remains in control — critical for trust and conversion.

Cold start strategy

For new users, start with lightweight defaults (household size + common recipes) and progressively personalize as history accumulates.

Failure modes

Design for being wrong: provide rationale, easy edits, and a fast “dismiss” so incorrect suggestions don’t create friction or resentment.

Design Decisions

Decision-centered design: what I chose, and what I intentionally did not build.

Decision 1

Target defaults, not education. Most solutions tell users to “waste less.” Smart Bundles changes what enters the home by default — where leverage is higher.

Decision 2

Bundle grouping over meal kits. Meal kits reduce choice but are operationally rigid. Bundles preserve flexibility while offering system-level guidance.

Decision 3

Learning loop as the differentiator. The product is not the first recommendation — it’s accuracy improving across orders with near-zero user effort.

Impact

A climate-positive feature that also strengthens the business.

Smart Bundles creates a rare alignment: reducing waste can also improve Amazon Fresh’s unit economics and retention. Bundling increases units per basket while lowering refund/complaint drivers tied to spoilage and overbuy regret.

User impact

Less spoilage, fewer “why did I buy this?” moments, and a cart experience that feels like planning support — not just checkout.

System impact

A closed-loop product that learns, improving portion accuracy across orders and reducing household-level over-purchase.

Business impact

Higher average order value via bundles, improved retention through “helpful defaults,” and stronger climate story aligned with Amazon’s net-zero commitments.

Next Steps

What I’d validate with time and resources.

1 · Validate reduction

Run an A/B test measuring spoilage proxies (returns, reorder gaps) and “portion satisfaction” from post-delivery feedback.

2 · Inventory-aware bundling

Connect bundle generation to near-expiration inventory to reduce upstream waste (warehouse + last-mile).

3 · Household onboarding

Design the trust and consent model: what data is used, how personalization works, and graceful opt-out controls.

Reflection

What this sprint sharpened.

Time pressure forced system-first prioritization.

With only 10 hours, I treated the prototype as proof of a product thesis, not a UI gallery. I focused on the minimum set of touchpoints needed to demonstrate a closed-loop system: discover → commit → learn.

AI-native products require a trust model.

Recommendation systems fail when users feel controlled or judged. The design pattern I’d push further is explainability: light rationale, easy edits, and fast dismissals so the system earns trust over time.
