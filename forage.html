Problem

For designers, the world is an asset library you can’t “Save As.”

Designers spot usable moments everywhere: a color on a flower, the curve of a ceiling, the motion of a pour, the type on a menu. Inspiration hits mid-movement, too fast to open an app.

What does get saved lands in a camera roll or a screenshots folder, cut off from why it mattered. It’s hard to find again, and harder to reuse.

Opportunity

How might we make capturing inspiration feel as natural as noticing it, so it’s still usable when the work starts?

Solution

One ecosystem, from noticing to making.

A wearable ring, creative intelligence, and a connected library keep the moment and make it useful later.

Core Experience

A gesture in the street becomes an asset in the library.

Wear the ring, turn on capture, outline what caught your eye, then choose what to keep. Each capture below shows the gesture, then the asset it becomes in the Field.

Wearable

Wear it where the gesture feels natural.

The ring sits on whichever finger you point with. A sculptural form and a range of finishes make it something you’d wear anyway, with capture always close at hand.

Activate

A shared start, then four ways to save.

Double tap the side of the ring to turn capture on. Draw a loose outline in the air around the object, then a second gesture picks the asset type.

Image capture

Tap to keep the composition.

Outline the poster, then tap once. The capture lands in the Field as an image, cropped to what you outlined and ready to download as a PNG.

PNG

Typography capture

Trace the lettering worth remembering.

Drag two fingers across the words. forage reads the text and matches the typeface, here Bodoni Poster Compressed, with similar fonts to compare before downloading.

Color capture

Two fingers, one color.

A two-finger tap keeps the color that caught your attention. The wall becomes a single swatch, #99B0BF, ready to copy into a file.

Keep the way it moves.

Hold, then drag slightly to record a short movement. The capture keeps the clip, a looping preview, and a timing curve for motion work.

Research

From a familiar frustration to a wearable interaction.

Research helped us decide what to preserve, who to design for, and where capturing should happen.

Validating the problem

The frustration went beyond our own camera rolls.

Discussions from other designers echoed what we had experienced: disrupted browsing, fragile collections, and saves disconnected from why they mattered. These qualitative signals pointed toward continuity between noticing, collecting, and reusing.

Target user

Chia sees potential in ordinary things.

Our persona notices a texture on a napkin, a color in the sky, type on a storefront, or the motion of a wave. She needs a way to carry those observations into future projects.

Goals

Turn everyday observations into useful creative references.

Behaviors

Notices type, composition, color, and movement while moving through the world.

Frustrations

Inspiration passes quickly; saved moments lose their connection to a project.

Needs

A quick capture and a library that preserves context for reuse.

Concept

The Invisible Sense

Inspiration often arrives unbidden. Research on creative inspiration describes it as an evoked motivational state; Carnegie Mellon’s design research explores how external stimuli support associations and idea generation. Our “invisible sense” is a design metaphor for that experience, not an additional biological sense. We connected it to the body through gesture and movement.

Wearable landscape

Why a ring?

Fleeting inspiration led us to explore capture that could stay with the body. We compared where existing wearable categories sit, and how they ask people to interact.

Glasses are an intuitive place for visual capture. We chose a ring for a tactile, discreet interaction through the hands, which designers already use to explore, point, and make.

Iterations

A shared scope kept the physical and digital work connected.

Research informed our product requirements: capture vocabulary, output types, and the library’s structure. That gave four people a common target within 72 hours.

Wearable rendering

Make the device feel at home on the hand.

The form evolved toward jewelry: a softer silhouette, placement that leaves the finger free to move, and finishes that suit different preferences. The renderings explored comfort and wearability; they did not validate a manufactured device.

Gesture fine-tuning

A distinct vocabulary, with less to remember.

We photographed hand poses and acted out alternatives to compare their ease and visual distinction. A shared selection step reduced the vocabulary to four asset choices, instead of a different interaction for every design tool.

Digital product

From a dashboard to a capture-first Field.

Figma Make helped turn the shared requirements into a working prototype. We brought captures forward, simplified the navigation, and separated browsing, organizing, and discovery. The interface became a place to return to references, with less attention spent on managing the system.

System Design

One capture moves through hardware, intelligence, and software.

The ring notices and selects, AI turns the selection into a usable asset, and the library keeps it findable. The ring and the AI pipeline are proposed; the web prototype shows the library.

Wearable hardware concept

Every part of the ring supports one moment of the gesture.

The camera sits on top of the band, facing out. Motion sensors and gesture detection read the hand; haptics confirm a save so you never look down. Component selection, battery life, and fabrication were outside the prototype’s scope.

AI creative intelligence · Proposed

Keep the useful information, not just the file.

Each gesture tells the AI what kind of asset to look for, so it can pull out the part a designer reuses: the subject without its background, the font behind the lettering, the exact color, the easing of a movement. The prototype shows these records; live recognition is proposed.

Privacy was part of the spec: no identifiable faces, capture turns off in sensitive places, and every record stays visible and editable. These are design requirements, not implemented safeguards.

Branding

An identity that feels collected, sketched, and kept.

Kinza and Lucy led the brand direction. Tactile forms and a hand-drawn wordmark made Forage feel personal. The bloom became the app icon; five expressive colors carry it from the ring to the stickers, while the interface gives the references room.

Reflection

The connection between parts is the experience.

Designing Forage meant carrying one intention from a hand movement to a reusable asset. The challenge was making that continuity visible within the time we had.

Takeaway · Human behavior

A natural interaction must fit the moment it serves. Capturing a fleeting observation is only useful if the designer can reconnect it to a project later.

Takeaway · Building with AI

A shared vocabulary made an ambitious system demonstrable in 72 hours. Figma Make accelerated the software, while clear boundaries kept the ring concept, simulated outputs, and working flows honest.
