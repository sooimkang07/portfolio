Service design · Mobile · 2025

neuk lets friends book a private screening room, so streaming becomes a night out again. One booking is the ticket, the door key, and the remote.

Role
Solo product designer
Scope
Service design
Product design
Branding
Strategy
Timeline
6 weeks
Fall 2025
Status
Concept

Problem

Everyone wants to watch together. Nobody has the room.

In cities like New York, apartments are small and shared, and the third spaces that could hold a group cost money and fill up fast. Friends still want to gather for the finale, the premiere, or the big game, but every place they can go gives something up.

Opportunity

How might we create a shared viewing experience that combines the comfort of home, the quality of a theater, and the social energy of going out?

Solution

Book a room. Check in with your phone. Stream anything.

neuk rents private screening rooms by the hour. One friend books a room sized for the group, everyone walks in with a QR ticket, and their phones become the remote for every streaming service.

Book

Filter by date, group size, and neighborhood. Every room shows its size, price, and open time slots before anyone commits.

Check in

The confirmation is a QR ticket. It opens the room’s door, so nobody waits at a front desk.

Stream

Scan the code on the screen and the phone becomes the remote. Everyone signs in to their own services, and the logins are wiped when the session ends.

Three room sizes, priced by the hour

Core features

One booking becomes the ticket, the key, and the remote.

Four moments take a group from “we should watch this together” to sitting in the room: discover, book, enter, and stream.

Feature 01 · Discover

Match the room to the group.

Filter by location, date, and room size. Room cards lead with big photos, and every size is explained before you choose.

Feature 02 · Book

Pick a slot, pay once, get a ticket.

The room page leads into evening time slots and a clear price breakdown. The confirmation becomes the group’s QR ticket.

Feature 03 · Enter

The ticket is the key.

A reminder lands an hour before. At the door, the QR ticket unlocks the room, and staff have already reset it for the group.

Feature 04 · Stream

The phone is the remote.

Scan the code on the screen to pair. The remote runs every streaming service and shows how much time is left, and past and upcoming bookings stay in the app for next time.

High fidelity

Twelve screens, from the first search to the remote.

The final design leads with the room. Dark screens keep the focus on the photos, and one blue accent marks what to tap next.

Research

People don’t go out to watch a movie. They go to be together.

I started with the market, then talked to people. A competitive analysis showed the gap between the places people already watch together. Interviews showed what keeps them from doing it more.

Competitive analysis

Every option gives something up. None has it all.

I compared movie theaters, apartments, and public spaces like bars and watch parties on what they offer and where they fall short. Theaters have the screen, apartments have the comfort and control, and bars have the energy. Nothing offered all of it in a private room.

User interviews

Small apartments, big groups, and a group chat that never decides.

A screener found six people who watch with friends regularly. I interviewed each one about how they watch, where, and what gets in the way, then sorted the notes into an affinity map.

5 of 6usually watch with friends in someone’s apartment
4+people at every watch party they described
1–4people fit comfortably in most of their living rooms
6 of 6wanted to hear more about renting a private room

01

A middle ground between home and theater

Home is comfortable but cramped and a chore to host. Theaters are immersive but rigid and impersonal.

02

Planning is the pain

Finding a time, getting everyone there, and splitting the cost kill the plan before it starts.

03

The people matter more than the movie

Time with friends is the draw, and many want a plan that isn’t built around drinking.

04

Worth it when it’s split

Price worried people until it was framed per person: a private theater night for about $20 each.

System thinking

Every tap in the app has a job in the room, so I designed both as one service.

Personas kept the work tied to real people. A system map set the app’s structure, a service blueprint lined up every screen with the staff and systems behind it, and a PRD set what the first version had to do.

Personas

Three people the service has to work for.

Each persona combines two interviewees with similar habits and needs.

The Social Host

“I love hosting. My space just isn’t super conducive to it.” She plans the themed nights, then cleans up after them.

The Cinephile

Waited nine years to see Interstellar on a big screen again. Wants the theater experience with friends, on their schedule.

The Budget Weekly Watcher

Watches the same show every week with the same friends. The ritual only works if it’s cheap enough to repeat.

System map

Five tabs, built around one loop.

The app is organized around the booking loop: find a room, get the ticket, pair the remote. Food and drink got its own tab in the map, but I scoped it out of the first version.

Service blueprint

One visit, from the first search to the room reset.

The blueprint follows a group through five stages and lines up what they do, what they see in the app and the room, what staff do behind the scenes, and the systems underneath. Food and drink lives here as a staffed service, ready for the app when it’s designed.

PRD

A PRD kept version one to the booking loop.

I wrote a PRD for “Watch Party Rooms,” the working title, to define the problem, the users, and what the first version had to do.

In version one

Browse and filter rooms, book and pay, a QR ticket that opens the door, and a phone remote.

Mapped for later

Food and drink ordering, split payments, and one in-room screen for every streaming service.

How it would succeed

Repeat bookings: the same friends booking the same room again, like karaoke or bowling.

Iterations & testing

People wanted cozy but elevated: the energy of going out with the comfort of home.

Before designing screens, I tested the vibe. That set up three visual directions, and a low-fidelity flow showed what the screens had to fix.

Concept testing

Participants sorted rooms and food into what was and wasn’t their vibe.

Like a card sort with pictures: each participant sorted rooms, food and drink setups, and app features into my vibe, could sometimes be my vibe, and not my vibe, and left comments on what drew them in. Open the deck ↗

01

Not extremes

People gravitated to a balance of social energy and homey comfort.

02

Cozy but elevated

Warm lighting and soft seating, with a screen worth leaving home for.

03

Not a club

Neon, party-like spaces felt too distracting or impersonal for most.

Directions

Three directions, each pushing one extreme.

Lume leaned into the quality of a theater, neuk into the comfort of home, and Streamie into the energy of going out. The final design keeps the best of the first two.

Low-fidelity testing

Testers wanted to see more, be told more, and control less.

Eight testers left notes screen by screen, from room cards that were too small to confusion about what happens after booking. Drag or scroll the strip to read them.

Lainey

Bigger room cards

“Room cards are too small.” Cards grew, and more of each photo shows.

Paul

Guidance after booking

“I’m confused on the process after booking it.” A reminder, the ticket, and a welcome in the room now walk the group through.

Grace

A simpler remote

“Just on/off works for me.” The full color wheel for the lights became a simple toggle.

Branding

Cinematic enough for a night out, cozy enough to feel like home.

I started by deciding what neuk is and isn’t, ran a naming sprint, and sketched the mark by hand. The system takes Lume’s dark palette, blue accent, and thin lines, and neuk’s name, rounded shapes, and warm cream.

What neuk is

Convenient, social, and cozy. It feels easy, like a party, and like home.

What neuk isn’t

Complicated, restrictive, or a big commitment.

The name

Nook, a corner that offers seclusion, plus 아늑한 (aneukhan), Korean for cozy. The k is drawn as a corner: your own nook in the city.

Next steps

Next, the parts of the night the app doesn’t cover yet.

The booking loop works end to end. Three questions are still open, and the service would need a way to launch.

How might we…

01

…imagine the food and drink ordering page?

It’s mapped in the service blueprint, with a kitchen and a runner behind it. The menu, cart, and order status screens come next.

02

…imagine a Splitwise-style way for friends to pay, right in the app?

So one friend doesn’t front the whole room and chase everyone for their share.

03

…imagine the movie screen’s interface, with every streaming service in one place?

So the group isn’t juggling logins and remotes once the lights go down.

If neuk launched, it would start in rooms that already exist

01

Rent before building

Pilot in karaoke rooms and older auditoriums during off-peak hours. Add a big screen and the neuk remote, and test demand without building anything.

02

Watch for repeat groups

The signal is the same friends booking again, not first-time curiosity.

03

Then build neuk rooms

Open purpose-built pods, lounges, and clubhouses in the neighborhoods that book the most.

Reflection

What began as a way to watch turned into a way to connect.

Even through screens, we still crave being side by side. Instead of letting screens keep us apart, neuk lets them bring us together, so friendships keep going even when our apartments can’t fit everyone.

Takeaway · People

People don’t want a bigger screen. They want a place to be together.

Takeaway · Service design

Booking was the obvious scope. Getting friends into one room was the real one.

Let’s make
something
worth using.
