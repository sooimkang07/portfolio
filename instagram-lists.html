Instagram Lists lets users organize their feeds by social context, while recommendations adapt to the context they choose. An independent feature concept.

Role
Product Designer
Timeline
7 weeks
December 2025
Platform
Instagram
iOS concept
Scope
Research
Product strategy
Systems design
Interaction design

Problem

Everyone you follow competes in one feed.

Opportunity

How might we give users control over what their feed prioritizes in each moment without sacrificing discovery?

Solution

Introducing Instagram Lists

Organize the people you follow into lists that reflect your different circles. Then browse Home, Stories, and Following within the context that matters in the moment.

Core flows

Lists live inside browsing, not beside it.

Defaults

Users start with Default Lists.

Home brings Feed, Stories, and Reels into one swipeable structure, with familiar default lists of All, Following, and Close Friends with no setup required.

Extend

Users can then extend this system into their own relationships.

Create custom lists around friends, school, creators, shops, or any other circle, then switch between them across Feed, Stories, and your Following.

Create

Creation happens where intent emerges.

Create a list wherever the need arises on Home and/or your Following, customizing its name, color, and accounts in one flow.

Edit

Editing happens where intent already exists.

Add or remove accounts while browsing, keeping lists up to date without interrupting the experience.

Research

Algorithms can predict relevance, not intent.

Platform audits & competitive analysis

Instagram has discovery and friends, but no way to choose between them.

I audited Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, and Snapchat. Instagram covers algorithmic discovery and friend-first interaction, but viewing modes are limited and there’s no intentional content filtering. Intentional viewing was the open space.

User interviews & affinity mapping

People mentally group who they follow, but Instagram puts everyone in one feed.

Think-aloud sessions with five active Instagram users revealed prolonged passive scrolling and distinct mental categories for friends, school, creators, and brands.

Explorations

Three directions, tested with 10 people. Only one fixed the root problem.

I tested three directions: easier access to feed controls, control over how you appear to others, and organizing who you follow into contexts. Only the third directly affected feed content.

System thinking

Users define the context. The algorithm ranks within it.

Lists don’t replace Instagram’s ranking. They focus it: people choose which social context matters right now, and Instagram decides what’s relevant inside it.

Before Lists

One feed mixed everything together.

Friends, creators, ads, and suggested posts are all mixed into the same feed. Each scroll jumps between unrelated content, making the experience feel fragmented and harder to land on content people actually care about.

After Lists

Every list is its own context.

The accounts people group together give Instagram context for what each list is about. That context shapes the recommendations, suggested posts, and ads within it, so each feed stays relevant to what someone chose to browse.

System mapping

Designed as a system,
not just a feature

I prioritized with MoSCoW, mapped every surface Lists touches, and traced the journeys to create, filter, and edit a list, so one choice holds across Home, Stories, profiles, and Following.

Potential impact

If validated, Lists could help…

  1. Users

    Focus on what matters most in each moment.

  2. Creators

    Reach the right audiences.

  3. Instagram

    Drive high-intent engagement without losing discovery.

Design decisions

Native to Instagram, and private by default

System fit

No new visual language

I built from Instagram’s existing visual language, testing list markers across Stories, profiles, and menus to ensure Lists felt distinct without adding noise or interfering with the platform’s existing icons, badges, and status signals.

Privacy

Lists only change what you see.

Sorting people is personal, so a list is visible only to the person who made it. No one is notified when they’re added, lists never show on anyone’s profile, and the people on a list see nothing different.

Next steps

Two questions remain before Lists could scale.

Do people organize their relationships into overlapping circles?

Research confirmed that users mentally group accounts they follow, but not whether those groups overlap. I’d investigate their mental models before introducing multiple-list membership.

Could AI make organizing hundreds of accounts easier?

Manually sorting large networks could hinder adoption. I’d explore AI-assisted grouping based on relationships and interests, letting users refine suggested lists rather than start from scratch.

Outcome

A concept grounded in real product thinking.

I presented this case study to Ian Goode (Former Head of Design at Meta), Darren Chin (Senior AI Product Manager at BCG X), and my professors Sally Chung (Founder of Designpreneurs & Co.), Dana Jefferson (Staff Product Designer, AI Imaging at Adobe), and Lauren Schlanger (Talent Manager & Creative Recruiter at Google Creative Lab). I received positive feedback on both my presentation and concept.

Reflection

People choose the context. The system works within it.

Takeaway 01 · User behavior

People already organize their relationships in their heads. Lists gives those existing circles a place in the product, so browsing can start with what matters in the moment.

Takeaway 02 · Designing with AI

Let people define the context, then let the recommendation system find what’s relevant within it. User choice can guide discovery without replacing it.

Let’s make
something
worth using.
