Instagram · 2025 · Add a Feature

Content-exposure architecture for intentional feeds

Role Product Designer
Timeline 3 weeks · Spring 2025
Team Solo
Skills Product Design
User Research

Overview

Instagram has 2B daily users but a single, undifferentiated content stream.

The feed algorithm decides what you see. Users can't group accounts, set context, or selectively share — meaning all social relationships are flattened into one exposure plane. Instagram Lists introduces a grouping layer that restores user agency: named collections that work bidirectionally for both consuming and sharing content.

System at a glance

Inputs User-defined account groups ("Close Friends," "Work," "Artists I follow")
Intelligence Lists filter the feed or restrict post audience — no ML required; user intent is the logic layer
Outputs Focused feed view or targeted post reach — intentional content exposure in both directions
Feedback loop Lists improve as users refine groups over time; shared content patterns reinforce group utility
Overview — Feature Concept

Problem

The feed optimizes for engagement. It doesn't optimize for meaning.

Instagram's architecture collapses all social relationships into a single algorithmic stream. Close friends and brand accounts compete for the same feed slot. Close Friends exists, but only for Stories. There's no way to batch-consume content from a meaningful subset of accounts — or to share selectively without a secondary account.

The system treats all social connections as equivalent inputs. The design challenge was to restore the distinctions users already hold in their heads.

Structural gap

No organizational layer between "following" and "close friends" — users have two settings for a relationship spectrum with dozens of gradations

Behavioral gap

Users maintain informal mental categories for who they follow, but the app provides no way to make those categories real

Workaround cost

6 of 8 research participants had created secondary "spam" accounts purely to control their audience — a significant signal of unmet need

Research

Users already have the mental model. The product just doesn't support it.

I interviewed 8 Instagram users (18–32) about feed management and sharing behavior, then audited comparable features across Twitter/X Lists, Facebook Friend Lists, and BeReal.

Research synthesis — affinity map

Insight: mental categories exist; the infrastructure doesn't

Every participant described informal groups: "people I actually care about," "colleagues I'm friendly with," "accounts I follow for inspiration." These categories were real and stable — but invisible to the product. Lists would make them legible.

Insight: selective sharing drives workarounds, not deletion

Users who felt they couldn't control their audience didn't stop posting — they created secondary accounts or moved to Stories-only. The desire to share selectively was persistent; the tools just weren't there.

Solution

Instagram Lists: bidirectional curation native to the feed.

Lists are named collections of accounts that operate in two directions: consuming (filter your feed to only show a list) and sharing (post content visible only to a list). They live as a filter layer inside the existing feed — not a separate tab, not a new surface.

The system principle: every relationship status users already assign in their head should have a corresponding organizational structure in the product.

Core Flows

Three interaction patterns, one system model.

Creating a list

From Following or a profile, users can add accounts to a named list. Lists are private by default. The creation flow is identical whether starting from scratch or appending to an existing list.

Filtering the feed

A horizontal scroll of list pills at the top of the feed lets users switch context instantly. Selecting a list shows only posts from accounts in that list — no algorithm, just the user's own grouping logic.

Feed filter UI — list pills

Sharing to a list

In the post audience selector, "Lists" appears alongside Public and Close Friends — giving users a named, persistent alternative to the binary audience options that exist today.

Design Decisions

Key tradeoffs and why.

Filter, not a tab — preserving feed context

Early explorations put Lists in their own tab. Testing revealed users didn't want to leave the main feed. A tab framing would have positioned Lists as "a different mode" rather than "your feed, filtered." The filter-pill pattern keeps users oriented in a familiar context.

Bidirectionality from day one — or it doesn't grow

A consume-only feature would have limited compounding value. Making Lists work for both viewing and sharing gives users a reason to invest in building accurate groups — which then makes the consumption side more valuable. The two behaviors reinforce each other.

Design decision — filter vs. tab exploration

Reflection

What I'd push further.

Features need two-way value to earn adoption.

A feature that only benefits the viewer or only benefits the sharer is half as compelling. The compounding value of Lists emerges from both directions working together — an important systems-level insight that should be front-loaded in any product pitch.

Familiar affordances lower the adoption barrier.

Borrowing visual language from Close Friends and Favorites meant users understood Lists without a tutorial — meeting their existing mental model rather than introducing a new one. Naming conventions matter as much as the interaction design.
