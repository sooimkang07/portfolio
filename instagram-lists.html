Overview

What if Instagram let you organize who sees what—and who you see?

Instagram has nearly 2 billion daily active users but its feed remains a single undifferentiated stream. Friends, acquaintances, brands, and creators all compete for the same attention with no way to group or prioritize them. This project proposes Instagram Lists: a feature that lets users create named lists of accounts, then filter their feed by list—bringing intentionality back to social media.

Overview — Feature Concept

Problem

The feed is noisy and passive by design.

Current Instagram architecture optimizes for engagement over intentionality. Users can't distinguish between close friends and casual follows in their main feed. The Close Friends feature only works for Stories, not posts. There's no way to batch-view content from a specific group of people.

Pain Point 1

Scrolling endlessly to find posts from people you actually care about

Pain Point 2

No organizational layer between "following" and "close friends"

Pain Point 3

Sharing to a specific sub-group requires a second account or DMs

Research

Learning from users and competitive patterns

I interviewed 8 Instagram users aged 18–32 to understand how they currently manage their feed and sharing habits. I also audited comparable features across Twitter/X Lists, Facebook Friend Lists, and BeReal's dual-camera system.

Research synthesis — affinity map

Key Insight 1: Mental models already exist

Every user I spoke with described maintaining informal mental categories for who they follow—"work contacts," "close friends," "artists I like"—but had no way to make those categories real inside the app.

Key Insight 2: Selective sharing is table stakes

Six of eight users had created alternate "spam" accounts purely for selective sharing. This is a significant workaround that Instagram Lists could eliminate.

Solution

Instagram Lists: intentional curation, native to the feed

Lists are named collections of accounts that work in two directions: viewing (filter your feed to only show a list) and sharing (post content visible only to a list). Lists live in the top navigation bar, alongside Following and Favorites.

Core Flows

Three primary user journeys

Creating a list

From your Following list or a profile page, users can add accounts to a new or existing list. Lists are private by default.

Filtering the feed

A horizontal scroll of list pills at the top of the feed lets users switch contexts instantly—similar to how Twitter Lists work but more visually integrated.

Feed filter UI — list pills

Sharing to a list

In the audience selector on post creation, "Lists" appears alongside Public, Friends, and Close Friends—giving users granular control over reach.

Design Decisions

Key choices and the reasoning behind them

Lists as a filter, not a separate tab

Early explorations put Lists in their own tab. Testing revealed users didn't want to leave the main feed context. Keeping lists as an in-feed filter felt native and less disruptive.

Two-way utility from day one

Viewing-only lists would have limited value. Making lists work for both consuming and sharing content means users have a reason to invest in building them out.

Design decision — filter vs. tab exploration

Reflection

What I learned

Features need two-way value to stick.

A feature that only benefits the viewer (filtering) or only the sharer (audience control) would be half as compelling. The magic of Lists is that both directions reinforce each other.

Familiarity lowers the adoption barrier.

Borrowing visual language from Close Friends and Favorites meant users intuitively understood Lists without a tutorial—meeting them where their mental models already lived.
