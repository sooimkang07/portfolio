Health wearable + app · Parsons AI Startup Design Hackathon · 2026

Acuity is an ecosystem for clarity before a cardiac crisis: a bracelet that senses, AI that learns your normal, and an app that tells you when to act. Designed in a 24-hour hackathon.

Problem

Half of people feel warning signs before cardiac arrest. Only one in five act.

Wearables already show heart rate, blood oxygen, and ECG, then leave the meaning to you. Is this serious, or just stress? Most people guess, and wait.

Solution

The bracelet senses, AI interprets, and the app tells you what to do.

Acuity learns your normal from the bracelet and the symptoms you log, flags sustained drift rather than one-off spikes, and turns it into one of three states. Each state comes with a plain next step.

Research

The most dangerous moment isn’t the emergency. It’s the uncertainty before it.

It started with stories of symptoms people couldn’t read: dizziness, chest discomfort, sudden fatigue. Kritika’s research kept landing on the same gap: people notice something is off, but can’t tell if it’s worth acting on. The people at highest risk are also the least equipped to decide.

Core flows

One question on every screen: do I need to act?

I designed the app around three moments: knowing where you stand, understanding an alert, and responding from the wrist.

App walkthrough

See the app in use.

Walk through the prototype, then explore the states and interactions below.

States

Three states answer one question: do I need to act?

Stable, Elevated, Emergent. A yes/no alert either over-triages or misses signals, so each state gets proportionate language and one clear action. Emergent is reserved for a significant change paired with symptoms.

Alert

An Elevated alert says what changed, what it means, and what to do next.

The change is measured against your baseline, not a population average, and explained in plain words. The main action is “Schedule evaluation.”

Wrist

Two buzzes, a pinch to see why, a wave to dismiss.

Responding shouldn’t mean navigating an app. A deliberate pinch opens a care card on your phone with the next step, holding the pinch keeps monitoring, and a wave dismisses the alert.

Design decisions

The people least equipped to decide became the people we designed for.

We started with a broad health monitor. Broad tools had shallow impact and missed the people who need monitoring most: those whose early heart symptoms don’t fit the textbook, who are often misdiagnosed and rarely buy wearables. So Acuity reaches high-risk patients through insurers and health systems, with real care behind every alert.

What we left out

Acuity stays narrow on purpose.

Iterations

The value became clear once Acuity stopped showing data and started saying what to do.

Early concepts led with detection and dashboards. Sensors alone also left gaps, because symptoms don’t always show up in numbers, so we added a way to log how you feel.

24 hours

Research, brand, and the app built side by side.

Kritika’s research set the problem, Lucy built the brand and deck, and I designed the app. Working in parallel meant the app’s states, the brand’s colors, and the pitch all told the same story by hour 24. AI compressed weeks of production into a single day.

Reflection

Named one of the standout concepts at the hackathon.

24 hours forced one thesis instead of a feature list. The bracelet isn’t the story. Deciding when to speak, and how urgently, is.

When AI can produce screens, imagery, and video in hours, the designer’s job shifts to judgment: what to cut, and what the system should say. Read the full write-up on Medium ↗

Takeaway

Health data is finally accurate; the value now is making it clear enough to act on before a crisis.
