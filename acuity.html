Health wearable + app · Parsons AI Startup Design Hackathon · 2026

Acuity is a conceptual AI-powered health monitoring ecosystem that connects a wearable bracelet with a mobile app to help people interpret physiological signals, understand changes in urgency, and navigate their next steps. Designed in a 24-hour hackathon.

Overview

The most dangerous moment in healthcare isn’t the emergency. It’s the uncertainty before it.

The setting

A 24-hour AI startup hackathon at Parsons.

Teams from Parsons and the University of Arizona had one day to define a problem, build a product, and pitch it to judges from Snap, IDEO, and Microsoft. I designed and built the app; Kritika led research and Lucy led the brand and deck.

The problem

Half of people feel the warning signs. Only one in five act.

Wearables already track heart rate and blood pressure, then leave the meaning to you. So people guess, and wait.

How might we

How might we help people know when something is wrong, and act before it’s too late?

Solution

A bracelet that senses, a gesture that answers, a phone that guides.

Acuity learns your normal and sorts any sustained change into one of three levels: Stable, Elevated, or Emergent. Each comes with one next step, and you confirm every action.

Core experience

Set up once. Then Acuity watches, and you respond.

Every flow is recorded from the working prototype.

Set up

Onboarding asks once, then calibrates for 14 days.

Account, health history, and an emergency contact, then Acuity learns your personal baseline.

Monitor

Three levels, three plans.

Every level explains itself with “View details.” Elevated and Emergent add a care plan. These levels and responses are proposed design logic, not clinically validated triage.

Respond

Answer from the wrist, without unlocking a phone.

Keep watching

Not ready to act? Watch closely instead.

Holding the pinch, or choosing “Continue monitoring,” starts a 24, 48, or 72-hour window. Acuity reassesses when it ends.

Track

Between alerts, see the trend.

Eight weeks of trends against your baseline, a history of escalations, and a report for your doctor.

Research

People don’t lack data. They lack a clear signal to act.

Market

Wearables are booming. The people most at risk still wait.

The market is set to more than double by 2030, yet cost keeps high-risk people from care.

Landscape

Wearables measure. Care services wait. No one sits in between.

Apple Watch, Oura, and WHOOP track continuously but leave the meaning to you. Telehealth and 911 guide action only once you’ve decided something is wrong.

Interviews

The barrier wasn’t data. It was doubt.

Every story described the same moment: feeling something, then explaining it away. One was personal: Kritika’s grandmother had jaw pain and nausea, was told to rest, and was gone within hours.

Persona

Maya isn’t negligent. She’s uncertain.

We designed for adults managing hypertension who delay care. Her journey is the design scenario Acuity is built around.

Business model

Insurers pay, so the people most at risk pay nothing.

An insurer-funded, per-member model reaches high-risk members at no cost to them. Fewer avoidable admissions pays for it.

Design decisions

Four choices that shape the system.

01

Why blood pressure, heart rate, and sleep?

Decision

Read each against the person’s own baseline, not a population average.

Why

Early strain shows as a trend across signals. All three can be sensed at the wrist.

Tradeoff

Cuffless wrist blood pressure needs cuff calibration and supports trends, not diagnosis.

02

Why three urgency levels?

Decision

Collapse every reading into Stable, Elevated, or Emergent, each with one next step.

Why

A yes/no alert either cries wolf or stays silent. A middle level can say “watch this.”

Tradeoff

Any tier can be wrong. Thresholds need clinical validation, and Stable never says “you’re fine.”

03

Why AI?

Decision

Use AI to model your baseline and judge whether small changes add up, not to show more data.

Why

Static thresholds ignore what’s normal for you. Continuous data predicts risk earlier.

Tradeoff

Models can be wrong, so care options stay rule-based and nothing happens without you.

04

Why wrist gestures?

Decision

Haptics announce a change; a pinch, hold, or wave answers it.

Why

When you feel unwell, unlocking a phone and navigating an app is the last thing you want.

Tradeoff

Gestures must be learned and can misfire, so every action also works on the phone.

System architecture

How hardware, AI, and software connect.

Signals flow one way. The bracelet and your context feed a model of your normal; the model turns sustained change into one urgency level; the app turns that level into a plan.

Explaining the model

The app shows its reasoning.

Settings explains, in plain language, how Acuity learns your baseline and decides when to escalate.

Validation & limitations

A connected experience, demonstrated. Clinical reliability, untested.

What we demonstrated

A working prototype, a product film, and a pitch in 24 hours.

Acuity was named one of the event’s standout concepts. We did not test it with patients or clinicians.

What remains open

Questions a real health product must answer first.

Cuffless wrist blood pressure needs cuff calibration and drifts, and we never tested real sensors. The tier thresholds are design logic, so false alarms and missed alerts would need clinical study. Gestures need alternatives for limited mobility or sensation, and health-data consent and escalation liability remain open.

What we’d do next

Validate the signal before polishing the screens.

Test wrist readings against cuff and ECG with clinicians, stress-test the tier thresholds for false alarms and missed alerts, then usability-test the levels and gestures with high-risk adults.

Reflection

Designing for health takes more than clear data. It takes earned trust.

The hard part wasn’t visualizing data. It was communicating uncertainty, choosing what to show first, and keeping the person in charge.

Takeaway · Health behavior

People don’t act on data. They act on clarity they can trust.

Takeaway · Designing with AI

When AI makes production cheap, judgment becomes the job.
