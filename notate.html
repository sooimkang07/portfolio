Chrome extension · 0→1 · 2026

Notate is a Chrome extension I designed, built, and launched to let users annotate live webpages and revisit their notes exactly where they left them.

Problem

We save what we find online, but lose the thinking behind it.

I interviewed four people who regularly save content from the web. Three common workarounds emerged, each introducing a different problem.

Opportunity

How might we help people capture their thoughts while browsing and revisit them without losing their original context?

Solution

Turn any live webpage into a space for notes and ideas.

Notate lets users attach annotations directly to webpage content, organize them into folders across websites, and return to the exact elements they annotated.

Core flows

One continuous workflow: annotate, organize, return.

Notate keeps the process of capturing, organizing, and revisiting ideas within the browsing experience, without requiring users to switch between tools or lose track of their sources.

Annotate

Leave notes directly on the content that inspired them.

Select any element on a live webpage, write an annotation, and save it alongside the original content.

Organize

Bring related notes together across websites.

Group annotations into color-coded folders, keeping ideas from different webpages organized by project or purpose.

Return

Revisit your notes exactly where you left them.

Select a saved annotation to reopen its webpage, or return to an existing tab, and jump directly to the element you annotated.

Onboarding

Guide new users from installation to their first annotation.

Chrome extensions can be easy to overlook after installation, so I designed a guided welcome page that walks users through opening and pinning Notate. Each step is confirmed before progressing, and the experience ends with users creating their first real annotation rather than exploring sample content.

Design decisions

Keeping notes connected to their source without disrupting browsing.

Three decisions shaped how Notate balances contextual note-taking with everyday browsing.

01 · Element-level anchoring

Attach notes to elements, not entire webpages.

Anchoring notes to specific elements preserves their context as users scroll. Notes remain draggable to avoid covering content.

02 · Note-level organization

Organize thoughts, not webpages.

Folders apply to individual notes rather than URLs, allowing users to group ideas across websites and projects.

03 · Non-intrusive revisiting

Revisit notes without interrupting browsing.

Read-only previews keep annotations out of the way until needed. Selecting a note jumps directly to its original element without re-entering annotation mode.

Build

I built Notate myself with Claude Code, and kept a decision log so the agents built what I decided.

Notate runs inside Chrome’s extension platform: a side panel, scripts that draw notes onto any webpage, local storage, and only the permissions it needs. I designed it and built it with coding agents, then shipped it to the Chrome Web Store.

The user journey, and what runs each step

Webpages change after you annotate them, so I designed for the element disappearing. The note stays near where you left it, with a warning, and Notate never guesses at a different element to attach it to. Onboarding follows the same rule: no tracking and no network calls. Nothing leaves your browser.

Launch

From a personal workflow problem to a published Chrome extension.

I designed, built, and published Notate on the Chrome Web Store, making it available for anyone to install and use. In its first few days, the extension received 21 installs without any promotion and its first public review, marking an early step toward validating the product with real users.

Reflection

Building Notate taught me to design for what happens after saving.

Taking Notate from an idea to a published Chrome extension challenged me to think beyond the moment of interaction, from how users retrieve saved information to how annotations behave on websites I don’t control.

Takeaway 01 · Designing for users

Saving information is only useful if people can recover the context that made it worth saving.

Takeaway 02 · Building a Chrome extension

Designing for live webpages means accounting for changing content, unpredictable layouts, and the browser’s own constraints.
